At 16, close to 80% of my hair was gone. Lumps of hair, I saw, on the shower drain until I was left with only 20%. In the beginning, I had suspected cancer, but it wasn’t. Still, no one knew why, not even the MDs of the leading hospitals whom I went to see because I was desperate for an answer, any answer.

Until then, I had been perfectly healthy, as I am now today.

But back then, I was scared. I was worried, and I was also a teenager, a phase when we are most sensitive about our looks. Thus, having to wear a wig in secret for over two years was truly harsh. Traumatic indeed.

So, every night, I used to sob silently in my room. Although to the outside world I was as active as ever, to be honest, for months I was devastated.

But I had to get up, if not for myself, then for my parents’ sake, because I wasn’t the only one crying. Later, I found out that my parents had also been crying, as they were so worried about their only child.

More than anything, I gained the strength to bounce back because I thought about my mother.

My mother is a concert pianist. I saw her perform in front of hundreds in the audience at posh venues like the Carnegie Hall of Korea, under a fancy spotlight.

But as her daughter, I know about the behind-the-scenes stories, the ordeals she experienced to get to where she is today. She knew loneliness and solitude, for one, as she had to leave for Austria all alone at only 15. She *had to*, as she was offered something she simply couldn’t refuse, a full scholarship from a top music school, and that precious once-in-a-lifetime opportunity to study under the tutelage of a maestro.

For the next 15 years, she lived alone in a foreign country where she knew no one, where she had to learn the language, starting with the A, B, and C of German.

Sometimes, she tells me stories of the hardships she experienced, how hard it was to be the only Asian in the entire town, for instance.

But then, my mother is a winner. Although shy, she approached townspeople first, who later became her friends. Also, she studied German day and night, devouring children’s books and animation videos, literally memorizing every word (now, she is fluent; she can almost pass as a German).

And for her career, she slept only three hours per day, practicing day and night.

Fifteen years later, she returned home with a PhD from a top music school.

But this is *not* the only reason I admire her. Instead, for over 20 years, she taught 100 students, changing their lives in some instances, like those who, despite their passion for piano, simply couldn’t afford quality lessons, or gifted students who never had the chance to discover they were hidden jewels. My mother gave her 100% because she saw herself in them. Even today, her students, who were in their early 20s and are now in their late 30s, continue to visit her to thank her.

This is my mother, and I am her daughter. As such, with my mother setting the bar so high, I simply couldn’t give up the fight, per se. Thus, I tried hard to push away negative thoughts that I knew wouldn’t help me. Instead, I focused on what I could do: I ate right. I went for all kinds of treatments and therapies. I worked out at least three times per week, almost religiously, no matter what.

As a result, my hair grew back. But it is not only the hair that grew. I grew as well. I now know what the saying, “Adversity makes a (wo)man” means. And I also got to know myself better – that I can overcome.

As such, I am taking this experience with me when I leave for your program, where I know I will face challenges. But I know I can do it – because yes, I am my mother’s daughter
